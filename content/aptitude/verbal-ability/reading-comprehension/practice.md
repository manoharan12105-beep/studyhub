## Passage 1: Sleep and Learning

Scientists have long known that sleep matters for health, but research over the last few decades has shown that it also plays an active role in learning. During sleep, the brain replays experiences from the day, strengthening the connections that form memories. Studies in which people learned a list of words and were then tested after either a night's sleep or an equal period awake have generally found better recall after sleep. Sleep does not simply protect memories from interference; it also seems to reorganise them, helping people notice patterns they missed while awake.

Despite this, many students cut back on sleep before examinations, believing that extra hours of study will help more. The evidence suggests the opposite: a tired brain absorbs new information poorly, and losing sleep after learning can weaken what was studied. The most effective strategy is to spread study over several days and protect a full night's sleep, especially the night after learning something new.

## Passage 2: The Work of City Trees

Trees in cities are often valued only for their beauty, but they provide services that are easy to overlook. A mature tree cools the air around it through shade and through the water that evaporates from its leaves, lowering nearby temperatures on hot days. Trees also slow rainwater as it falls, reducing the load on drains during storms, and their roots help hold soil in place.

These benefits are not shared evenly. Wealthier neighbourhoods often have far more tree cover than poorer ones, which means that the residents least able to afford air conditioning are frequently the ones exposed to the most heat. Some cities have begun to treat tree planting as public infrastructure rather than decoration, setting targets for canopy cover in every ward. Planting is only the first step, however: young trees need years of watering and protection before they can deliver the benefits that mature trees provide.
