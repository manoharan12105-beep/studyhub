# Cause and Effect

## Concept

Two statements describe events. You must decide how they are connected: does one cause the other, are both results of the same underlying cause, or are they unrelated?

A **cause** is an event that makes another event happen; the **effect** is what results. The cause comes first in time and explains the effect.

Answer options are standard:

| Option | Meaning |
|--------|---------|
| A | Statement I is the cause and statement II is its effect |
| B | Statement II is the cause and statement I is its effect |
| C | Both statements are independent causes |
| D | Both statements are effects of independent causes |
| E | Both statements are effects of some common cause |

## Rules

### Testing "I causes II"

Ask: **"Did II happen because of I?"** If the answer is a direct, natural "yes", I is the cause.

- I. Heavy rain lashed the city all night. II. Low-lying areas were waterlogged in the morning. → Waterlogging happened because of the rain → **A**.

### Testing the reverse

Swap the roles. Many questions put the effect first to test this.

- I. Vegetable prices rose sharply. II. Unseasonal rain destroyed crops. → Prices rose because crops were destroyed → **B**.

### Common cause

Neither statement explains the other, but both are easily explained by one event that is not mentioned.

- I. Banks in the city were closed today. II. Post offices in the city were closed today. → Neither caused the other; both point to a common cause such as a public holiday → **E**.

### Independent causes

The statements are unrelated — no reasonable single event explains both and neither explains the other.

- I. The price of gold rose this week. II. A cricket team won a series this week. → **D**.

Option C ("independent causes") describes two statements that are each causes of separate things; it is rarely the answer and should be chosen only when both statements are clearly causes, not results.

## Solving Approach

1. Put the two events in time order — the cause must come first.
2. Test "II because of I" and "I because of II".
3. If neither works, ask whether one unmentioned event would naturally produce both (common cause, E).
4. If the events are unrelated, choose D.
5. Prefer the **direct, immediate** link; do not build long chains of maybes.

## Problem Patterns

### Pattern 1: Natural events and consequences

Rain, floods, fog, snowfall → waterlogging, cancellations, stranded travellers. Usually A or B.

### Pattern 2: Policy and response

Tax cuts, price changes, new rules → changes in sales, behaviour. Usually A or B.

### Pattern 3: Simultaneous closures or price rises

Several institutions closed or several prices rising together → usually E (common cause).

### Pattern 4: Unrelated news

Events from different domains with no plausible link → D.

## Common Mistakes

- Reading the statements in the order printed and assuming I is the cause.
- Inventing long causal chains to connect unrelated events.
- Choosing "independent causes" (C) when the statements are actually effects.

## Placement Tips

- Say the link aloud: "Y happened **because** X happened." If it sounds natural, you have the direction.
- When two similar things happen at the same time (closures, price rises), think "common cause".

## Key Takeaways

- The cause comes first and explains the effect; test both directions.
- Similar simultaneous effects → common cause (E); unrelated events → independent causes (D).
- Choose the direct link, not a speculative chain.
