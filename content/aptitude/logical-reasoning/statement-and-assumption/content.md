# Statement and Assumption

## Concept

An **assumption** is something the speaker takes for granted without saying it. It is **implicit** in a statement if the statement would not make sense — or the speaker would not have made it — unless the assumption were true.

This differs from a [conclusion](../statement-and-conclusion/content.md): a conclusion is something that **follows from** the statement; an assumption is something the statement **rests on**.

**Example:** A notice says "Please use the stairs during the power cut." The writer assumes that lifts do not work without power and that people are able to use the stairs. Neither is said, but the notice is pointless without them.

Answer options are standard:

| Option | Meaning |
|--------|---------|
| A | Only assumption I is implicit |
| B | Only assumption II is implicit |
| C | Either I or II is implicit |
| D | Neither I nor II is implicit |
| E | Both I and II are implicit |

## Rules

### The negation test

Reverse the assumption and ask whether the statement still makes sense.

- "Lifts work normally during a power cut" → then the notice is pointless → the original assumption **is implicit**.
- "No other toothpaste whitens teeth" (for an advert "Buy XYZ for whiter teeth") → negated: "other toothpastes also whiten teeth" — the advert still makes sense → **not implicit**.

### Usually implicit

- **Advertisements and appeals** assume people want the benefit offered and that the message can influence them.
- **Notices and instructions** assume people will read them and can follow them.
- **Decisions and plans** assume the action is feasible and will help achieve the stated aim.
- **Advice** assumes the advised action is beneficial.

### Usually not implicit

- Assumptions with **extreme words** — *only, all, always, never, completely* — unless the statement itself uses them.
- Statements of the **outcome** (that is a conclusion or prediction, not an assumption).
- Claims about **other people, places or products** not mentioned.
- Possible motives that the statement does not need.

## Solving Approach

1. Identify the speaker's purpose: inform, persuade, instruct, decide, advise.
2. Ask what must be true for that purpose to make sense.
3. Apply the negation test to each assumption.
4. Reject assumptions with unsupported extreme words or outside information.

## Problem Patterns

### Pattern 1: Advertisement

**Example:** "Buy XYZ toothpaste for whiter teeth." I. People want whiter teeth ✓. II. No other toothpaste whitens teeth ✗ (extreme, about other products) → **Only I**.

### Pattern 2: Notice or instruction

**Example:** "Please use the stairs during the power cut." I. Lifts do not work during a power cut ✓. II. People can use the stairs ✓ → **Both**.

### Pattern 3: Policy decision

**Example:** "The government will add more buses on city routes to reduce traffic congestion." I. More people will use buses if more are available ✓ (otherwise the plan cannot reduce congestion). II. Congestion will disappear completely ✗ (extreme; an outcome) → **Only I**.

### Pattern 4: Advice

**Example:** "To stay healthy, eat green vegetables daily." I. Green vegetables help keep people healthy ✓. II. Only green vegetables keep people healthy ✗ → **Only I**.

## Common Mistakes

- Choosing assumptions that are true in real life but not needed by the statement.
- Confusing assumptions with conclusions or predicted outcomes.
- Accepting assumptions with "only", "all" or "completely".

## Placement Tips

- Ask "why would anyone say this?" — the answer usually reveals the implicit assumption.
- Use the negation test whenever unsure; it is quick and reliable.
- Advertisements, notices and decisions follow predictable patterns — learn them.

## Key Takeaways

- An implicit assumption is what the statement takes for granted.
- Negation test: if negating it makes the statement pointless, it is implicit.
- Reject extreme words, outcomes and outside information.
